OpenSats was founded in 2020 with one goal in mind: to provide sustainable funding for those who are working on free and open-source software that makes Bitcoin flourish. From the get-go, the organizational structure was set up to avoid two failure modes that often plague non-profit organizations: waste and corruption.

To achieve this impossible task, three rules were put in place:

- [No benevolent dictator](#no-benevolent-dictator)
- [No cut from donations](#no-cut-from-donations)
- [No monetary incentives](#no-monetary-incentives)

From an operational standpoint, we agreed on the following:

- [No physical location](#no-physical-location)
- [No free lunch (no exceptions)](#no-free-lunch-no-exceptions)
- [No perfect system](#no-perfect-system)

That's a weird set of constraints, so let's explain both the rules and the operational constraints in more detail.

## No Benevolent Dictator

To ensure that OpenSats doesn't corrupt over time, an unusually large board is at the center of its governance structure. Nine board members have to agree on funding decision by majority vote. Structural and organizational changes require supermajority approval. As stated in our [FAQ](/faq#how-did-you-choose-the-board) and [about page](/about#board-of-directors), we don't want a single person to be in charge of the organization. The downside of this structure is that things move slowly sometimes. The upside is that OpenSats is hard to change and that the influence any single person has on decisions is minimal.

The sheer volume of applications puts a strain on the organization, especially the board. To deal with that scaling challenge we introduced volunteer committees to help with application evaluations, especially on the technical dimension.

When we assembled our first technical subcommittee, the nostr subcommittee, we announced its members publicly. Shortly after, some members received blackmail and bribes, such as "if you approve my application I will split the grant money with you" or "if you don't approve my application I will make public what you told my in confidence." Consequently, we decided to be public about the structure of our committees and our [decision making process](/selection), while protecting the identity of the individual members of said committees. That said, every committee member is free to disclose that they are part of an OpenSats committee.

All committee members are volunteers. The board is in charge of the final decision, taking the technical evaluation of each committee into account. Each application has to pass technical review (majority approval by the technical committee) and board review (majority approval by the board). The overarching goal is to set up structures and processes so that no single person has an outsized influence over funding decisions.

## No Cut from Donations

To minimize organizational bloat as much as possible, OpenSats [doesn't take a cut](/faq#are-you-really-100-passthrough) from donations. Our [operations budget](/funds/ops) is funded separately, meaning that we have to figure out how to keep the lights on independent of how many donations we receive for open-source funding.

Considering the size and scope of our grant programs, OpenSats is relatively lean operationally: a team of 3 people plus one board member to oversee operations. No office, no travel expenses, no other expenses that might be "normal" for an operation of this size.

> Unlike most charities, OpenSats doesn't take a percentage of any donations for our operational budget. This means that our small operation budget is funded from donors like you.
>
> <cite>—[opensats.org/about](/about#operations)</cite>

## No Monetary Incentives

As mentioned above, our committee members are volunteers, lending their technical knowhow so that grant applications are evaluated with the rigor and expertise they deserve. In addition to our technical committees we've had various groups of volunteers and [supporters](/support) that helped with our website, our blog posts, and with the initial tasks required to get OpenSats up-and-running in the early days.

Board members are volunteers too, as mentioned in the [board of directors section in our about page](/about#board-of-directors).

> Board members are not compensated for their board member duties, and do not benefit from donations given to our funds. Board member duties are performed on a volunteer basis.
>
> <cite>—[opensats.org/about](/about#board-of-directors)</cite>

To hold ourselves accountable, we publish our [bylaws](/docs/bylaws.pdf), our [grant agreement](/docs/agreement.pdf), the [meeting minutes](/transparency#meeting-minutes) of our board meetings, as well as our [financial statements](/transparency#financial-statements) online. In addition to that, all of our grants are announced publicly, and we show the cumulative amount of sats sent for each project that is listed on our [projects page](/projects).

There is an inherent tradeoff balance between privacy and transparency. From the first grant wave onwards, multiple grantees have asked us to not announce grant details, specifically the [individual grant amounts](/faq#do-you-disclose-grant-amounts-publicly), since this information would endanger them or their family.

Our grants are awarded worldwide, independent of the applicants background or socioeconomic status. In some areas of the world, unfortunately, the news of someone being funded for their work immediately endangers them. We want to do what's best for our grantees, hence we made the decision to announce every grant while not disclosing [grant amounts](/faq#do-you-disclose-grant-amounts-publicly) directly. Grantees are free to disclose these details of their [grant agreement](/docs/agreement.pdf), and some of them do.

## No Perfect System

Are our processes perfect? Of course not, no system ever is. Anyone or anything that makes judgement calls will get things wrong. False positives and false negatives are unavoidable. In the case of OpenSats, that means that we sometimes fund projects that shouldn't be funded, and sometimes fail to fund projects that should. We strive to get better at what we do with each funding cycle and, in general, want to err on the side of funding (which is to say: to give people the benefit of doubt).
