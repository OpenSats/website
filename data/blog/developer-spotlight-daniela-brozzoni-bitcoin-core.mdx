Imagine being 19 years old. You are still in high school, your
future lies in front of you with all the confusion and noise of
your teenage years. Google comes knocking at your door: they want
to hire you for an internship, your first paycheck would say
something like 7000€/month. What would you say? Would you accept?

Well, Daniela Brozzoni said no. Of course she did, otherwise we
would not be here telling her Bitcoin story, right?

She was contacted directly from the recruiting department of Big G.
It was April, and she hadn’t finished her last year of high school
yet.

> At the time I was very involved in computer science and algorithm
> competitions, and a lot of the people around me were following a
> pretty standard path where Google was basically the dream. You leave
> your small town in Italy, move to some exciting big city, work in
> one of those beautiful offices you see online, and you've made it.

She remembers doing a couple of rounds of interviews with Google’s
emissaries. After the initial excitement wore off, she started to
think that something might be off, wondering why they had contacted
her instead of other peers, who, as she recalls, used to obtain
better results but had never heard from Google.

Google was talking a lot about diversity in hiring at the time, and
I couldn't really get that thought out of my head. I didn't want to
be hired because I made some percentage look better. I wanted
someone to care about my brain, my work, and what I could actually
contribute.

That was not the only thought in Daniela’s mind, though. At the same
time, due to her initial fall in the Bitcoin rabbit hole, she was
starting to really care about the concept of privacy and the whole
philosophy attached. As everyone knows at this point, Big G is not
exactly what we may call a privacy-oriented company. She was
starting to understand how much data companies collect, what they
know about people, how little control users have over that
information, and all the power that follows.
—

> I've liked computers for as long as I can remember. I think I got my
> first one when I was five or six, maybe even earlier. Obviously at
> that age I was mostly playing games and using the internet, but I
> also remember messing around with a bit of HTML.

Daniela’s love for technology – and computers specifically – came at
a very young age. She recalls being 5 years old when she got her
first computer and started tinkering with it, mostly for games and
the internet,  but also for a bit of [HTML].

However, her first, real encounter with computer science came when
she was 13 years old, when her dad came home with an [Arduino][arduino] board.
He needed help – he wasn’t a coder and the whole documentation was
in English – and Daniela was the right person to do so.

> I remember coding little things with him and finding it so fun. It
> felt almost like talking to a machine. If I press this button, you
> turn on this LED. I could decide what I wanted the computer to do
> and then figure out how to make it happen.

She loved that work so much that she decided to study computer
science in high school. “My background is actually pretty
straightforward. I liked computers as a kid, discovered programming,
and kept going,” she says.

She isn’t only into computers, though. Daniela is an avid reader,
and she doesn't care too much to be consistent on what she reads:
“ like having both the ‘I am learning something’ books and the ‘I am
just having fun’ books going at the same time.”  She also loves
videogames, which she sees as one of the things that helps her
unwind after stressful days at work.

Although she made computers the center of her life – or precisely
for that reason – Daniela loves to hand write as much as she can.
“I take many handwritten notes, both for work and whenever I'm
learning something new. I like drawing diagrams and using different
pens and inks, which makes learning new stuff even more fun,” she
says.

—
> I first got interested in Bitcoin because of maths and cryptography.

It was during the third year of high school that Daniela first heard
about Bitcoin. Most people enter the space because at first they are
convinced that they could become rich in the blink of an eye.
Daniela, on the other hand, was attracted by its mathematical and
cryptographic properties.

A friend of hers explained how Bitcoin uses
[Elliptic Curve Cryptography][secp256k1] for key generation. That hook got
her in the rabbit hole. “The first book I read was [Mastering Bitcoin][mastering bitcoin],
and once I started understanding how all the pieces fit together, I basically
found the entire thing interesting.”

The more she dove into Bitcoin, the more she felt pulled towards it.
She started to understand that Bitcoin wasn’t just a technological
marvel, but that to really grok it you’d need to dive into multiple
disciplines – economics, philosophy, sociology.

In particular, she was fascinated by game theory and incentives.
She recalls discovering Bitcoin during the [Blocksize Wars][block wars]. She
wasn’t that deep in the technical arguments at the time, but she
“was fascinated by the way people talked about incentives. What will
this actor do? What can they do? What happens if people disagree?
How does the system stay robust when nobody is really in charge?”

Her interest in open-source came as a direct consequence of her fall
into the Bitcoin and privacy rabbit holes.

> The idea that the code was public and anyone could inspect it was
> really appealing to me. If you use a closed-source application
> that's collecting very personal information, you don't really know
> what it's doing with that data, who it's sharing it with, or how the
> software is making decisions. With open source, at least you have
> the possibility of looking.

—

> That was the point where I realized, oh, this can actually be a job.
> I could do this too.

In Italy, there is a mandatory program for high school students
called “alternanza scuola-lavoro” (school-work alternation), where
students get internships at companies to start getting their feet
wet in a real work environment. Daniela got an internship at a very
particular company – which isn’t active anymore – called [BHB Network].
Founded by the current president of Plan B Network,
Giacomo Zucco, BHB Network focused on bringing together the best
minds in the Bitcoin industry.

> I met this group of people working together on something they
> genuinely cared about. It felt much more collaborative and alive,
> and honestly just more fun. I think that influenced what I wanted
> from my career quite a lot.

During that brief experience, Daniela started attending her first
meetups and met several people who were actually working on the
protocol, and that made everything concrete. Most importantly, she
realized that working in Bitcoin could be feasible, not just as a
hobby or a side project, but as an actual job.

> I had found a community of people who were extremely passionate
> about what they were building, and I wanted to work on something
> that mattered to me, around people who felt the same way.

—
> Obviously Google is pretty far from where I was starting to land
> philosophically, so that also made the idea of working there feel
> less and less natural.

At about the same time she was interviewing with Google, that same
friend that introduced her to Bitcoin – together with the people she
met at BHB Network – got Daniela an interview at Blockstream. She
got the job.

Put in front of the choice – a fancy and highly paid job at BigG, or
an opportunity at a company that deeply resonated with her views –
Daniela’s decision became clearer.

> Once I had those two paths in front of me, the decision became much
> easier. On one side was Google, the big name, the fancy offices, all
> of that. On the other side I could work on Bitcoin, which I found
> both technically fascinating and genuinely important.

At the time, she had also started university. However, she got so
involved in the job she was doing on Bitcoin that she actually never
attended a single class and soon decided to drop out to continue on
the path of [Free and Open-source Software][foss] (FOSS).

Daniela has been an OpenSats grantee [since January 2025][grant post]. She got a
grant to work on [Bitcoin Core][core], the reference implementation of a
Bitcoin node. Between her internship at Blockstream and her current
work, Daniela worked on several projects, both in the open-source
space & Bitcoin companies.

She started working on a Blockstream wallet called Green, then moved
to [Braiins] to work on mining firmware. She then joined a startup
called [Revault], which focuses on providing wallet solutions to
companies and organizations.

While working at Revault, she started contributing to the
[Bitcoin Dev Kit][bdk] (BDK). BDK is a library that abstracts all the
complexities of managing a Bitcoin wallet, providing a ready-to-use
solution to build upon.

That’s when she got her first grant from [Spiral]. She became a stable
maintainer of BDK, working on several features like hardware signers
integration and coin selection.

> I'd already been contributing for a while, and I basically reached
> out and asked if they would fund me to work on it full-time. They
> said yes, and suddenly I could spend all my time working on open
> source. That was really cool.

There was something that Daniela wanted to do, though. She wanted to
become a contributor to Bitcoin Core.  Once her Spiral grant came to
an end, she started working full-time on Core. She decided not to
apply for funding immediately, though, so she went on for some time
using only her savings.

> When you first start working on Core, you can put in a huge amount
> of effort without having much visible output. So much of your time
> is spent reading, learning, reviewing, and trying to understand how
> everything works. I wanted to give myself some time to do that
> before applying for a grant.

When her financial situation changed unexpectedly, as it usually
happens, she had two choices: “apply for funding earlier than I'd
planned, or start looking for another job.” The latter choice was
not appealing to her, so she decided to apply for an OpenSats grant.

> I remember being very honest in my OpenSats application. I told them
> that I was still new to Core, that I didn't have a huge amount of
> visible output yet, and that I needed a small grant to support me
> through this time, because I couldn't keep living off my savings
> forever.

She got the grant. “The grant basically meant I didn't have to stop.
I could keep spending my time learning, reviewing, and working on
Core instead of looking for another job.” She was going to do
exactly what she wanted.

—
> But I think technology is what brought me to Bitcoin, while the
> community is probably what made me stay.

Working remotely from your own desk is a solitary endeavor. Most of
your interactions are made through a screen and sometimes you do not
even know the face of the person you are talking to. Thus, it’s
important to always remember that you are actually working on
something bigger than yourself and that you are part of a strong and
passionate community.

Daniela knows this and makes sure to plan some time to spend amongst
Bitcoiners. “I try to be intentional about going to conferences,
planning trips, or just showing up at local meetups whenever I can,”
she says.

So, when she is discouraged, she tries to find new energy by
spending time with Bitcoiners, those people that, she knows, share
the same vision: “Just being around people who care about the
ecosystem reminds me why I'm doing the work in the first place.”

—

> What surprised me was that almost everyone also made a point of
> telling me about the personal impact I'd had on them or on the
> project.

Some of Daniela’s proudest moments dealt with techy stuff. The first
grant with Spiral, the grant from OpenSats to work on Core, and even
finding small and subtle bugs in BDK.

However, the one she loves the most has nothing to do with code. It
happened when she told people she was leaving BDK to work on Core.
“Until then, I'd mostly measured my contribution in code. Things I
had built, bugs I'd fixed, reviews I'd done,” she recalls.

Her peers, though, did not care about that too much. They cared more
about letting her know the impact that she had on them personally.

> That was probably the first time I really understood that being a
> good person to work with is also a contribution. You can make
> someone's work easier, help them when they're stuck, or just make a
> project a nicer place to spend time.

—
> I think there are some scary times ahead, but hopefully we'll come
> out of this stronger. The odds are a bit stacked in favor of
> attackers: they can find a vulnerability and exploit it, while
> defenders need to find it, triage it, fix it, coordinate a release,
> and get that fix to users without tipping off the attackers.

At the time of writing, Daniela is also busy helping the [Red Team][red team].
“I mostly do outreach for the Red Team: once the clankers have found
something, I make sure it gets to the right teams,” she says. It is
certainly a stressful job. Disclosing vulnerabilities and making
sure that the information gets to the right people is not something
to take lightly.

Daniela thinks that Bitcoin is just an early preview of what will
hit the rest of the world’s infrastructure. She thinks that “the
incentive to attack it is very direct: if you successfully attack
Bitcoin infrastructure, there can be money at the end of it
immediately.” She is hopeful for the future, though, and there are
many lessons to be learned.

—

> I really enjoy helping other people move their work forward, so I
> spend a lot of time reviewing. I read people's code, think through
> the changes they're proposing, give feedback, and try to help good
> work get over the finish line.

Daniela is now a full-time Bitcoin Core contributor, and she is
happy about where this path is leading her. She is getting more
comfortable with the codebase, and she is now focusing on reviewing
other people’s code.

She is also busy with her own research projects, focused on making
the peer-to-peer side of Bitcoin more private. A node can open
connections on different networks – for example, Tor and clearnet –
but even if some of those running under Tor are private, the ones
running on normal traffic can be linked to those private ones.
Daniela’s effort is focused on making those links more difficult.

> I still feel very early in my journey, so I don't really have some
> grand vision of exactly what I'll be doing five or ten years from
> now. I genuinely don't know, and I'm fine with that.

She doesn’t have big plans for the future yet. She just knows that
she wants to continue walking the Bitcoin and open-source paths.

> I know that I love working on Bitcoin, I love learning, and I like
> working with people and helping them do good work. So for now I
> mostly want to keep doing those things, get better at them, and see
> where that takes me.

—
