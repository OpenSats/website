<blockquote>
<p style="text-align: left;">Growing up, I had weekly or bi-weekly room checks.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">Why?... I couldn’t tell you.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">Asking a ten year old boy to keep his room clean is a futile exercise. Usually, I’d get a few minutes’ notice before the inspection… so into the closet everything went. Layer upon layer of junk, compressing like sediment, each level carbon-dated to the room check conducted days prior.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">About once a month, the dreaded request would come: “Clean out your closet… we’re giving away anything you don’t need.” Every purge ended the same way: the wood-paneled Chrysler Town &amp; Country minivan sagging under a hundred pounds of clothes, toys, and other unwanted stuff.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">I’ve been told there are five love languages, but I’d argue there are at least six… my mom’s was cubic feet. She loved us in bulk… that’s how I thought of it, anyway. We lived in a house filled and refilled with things nobody asked for.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">We weren’t ungrateful, but truthfully, all I needed at the time were my Pokémon cards, my Nintendo 64, and a few Lunchables.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">The surplus always ended up just down the street, at the local Goodwill. After the 20th or so sacrificial closet ritual, I finally inquired about Goodwill’s business model and learned we were “donating” things to people who needed them more. We were helping a charity!</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">I was a skeptical ten year old… so questions were asked. “If we’re donating for free, why do they sell our stuff? Who is getting the money?”</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">Eventually, I learned the Goodwill execs running the “charity” were making hundreds of thousands of dollars a year. At the time, my entire financial world was begging for quarters to buy watermelon Airheads at the local pool. So, learning that someone was middlemanning my donated clothes for $500,000 a year felt like a betrayal. That's two million Airheads. Every year.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">That broke something in me.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">My vocabulary wasn’t vast at the time, but I had understood “charity” to mean giving to others, out of love, for nothing in return. This particular “charity” took my stuff, took my closet labor, sold both at a markup, and paid themselves first. That ruined the giving… for me at least.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">I went on strike after that.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">That childhood memory has stuck with me. The lessons I learned from that charity’s flaws became the principles OpenSats was built on.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">Most FOSS projects start as passion projects. Small, niche tools built for small, niche needs. Every once in a while one of these tools is so valuable that it outgrows its humble origins and evolves into load-bearing infrastructure for entire ecosystems.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">Downloads are free. Development and maintenance are not. More adoption leads to more feature requests, security fixes, and upkeep. In our increasingly AI-dominated landscape, issues and pull requests arrive automated and exponential. Demand from the code rises, but the supply of developers does not.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">The tragedy in FOSS isn’t that the code disappears.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">It’s that the person maintaining it does.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">Many developers eventually leave their projects… not for something they love more, but for something that pays the bills. The highest bidders for their time are usually the companies that benefit most from open source and contribute the least to its survival. The worst actors acquire the people most qualified to build tools that could make them obsolete. Brain drain away from the most accessible, beneficial, and desperately needed projects.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">That’s why OpenSats exists: to fight that brain drain and keep the best people building in the open.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">When we started, we looked at the traditional charity model… the one that takes a cut, pays itself first, and forwards only what is leftover to the cause. We built something that can’t work that way.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">OpenSats was built as a lean, 100% passthrough non-profit. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/funds/ops"><strong><u>Operations are funded separately</u></strong></a>, so we never take a percentage of the donations intended for developers. Most charities skim 15-30% of donations for operations. We take nothing. Every bit of every donation to our open-source funds goes to our grantees.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">We also wanted <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/blog/avoiding-single-points-of-failure"><strong><u>no single points of failure</u></strong></a> and no concentrated power. No benevolent dictator. Every important decision is made by a nine-person board of unpaid volunteers. We structured the whole organization so no individual has undue influence, and to prevent the kind of self-dealing that once broke my trust in my local Goodwill.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">I’m proud of what our grantees have built, everyone who has contributed, and the organization we’ve put together.</p>

<blockquote><p style="text-align: left;"><strong><em>The purpose of life is to discover your gift. The work of life is to develop it. The meaning of life is to give your gift away.</em></strong></p><p style="text-align: left;">— David Viscott</p></blockquote>

<p style="text-align: left;">That’s what FOSS developers do every day.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">Our job is to make sure they can keep doing it.</p>

<p style="text-align: right;">— <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/about/abitcoinperson"><strong><u>Ben Price</u></strong></a><br>Founder of OpenSats</p>

<h2 style="text-align: left;"><strong>Rooted in Resilience</strong></h2>

<p style="text-align: left;">For most people, October 31 is a day for dressing up and trick-or-treating. For bitcoiners, it's Whitepaper Day: the day that Satoshi Nakamoto introduced Bitcoin to the world. He <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://www.metzdowd.com/pipermail/cryptography/2008-October/014810.html"><strong><u>summarized his invention in a single line</u></strong></a>: &quot;a new electronic cash system that's fully peer-to-peer, with no trusted third party.&quot; In the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/bitcoin.pdf"><strong><u>whitepaper</u></strong></a>, he went on to describe the benefits of such a system: &quot;A purely peer-to-peer version of electronic cash would allow online payments to be sent directly from one party to another without going through a financial institution.&quot;</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">Money that you can send from one computer to another, without going through <em>any</em> institution—without relying on trust. Bitcoin is resilient precisely because it doesn't rely on trusted third parties. Its design avoids central points of failure, making it an anti-fragile network, not unlike the mycelial networks we know from the natural world.</p>

<blockquote><p style="text-align: left;"><strong><em>Briefly, and non-technically, Bitcoin is the first example of a new form of life. It lives and breathes on the internet. […] It lives because anyone, anywhere, can run a copy of its code. […] It lives because if any one copy is corrupted it is discarded, quickly and without any fuss or muss.</em></strong></p><p style="text-align: left;">— <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://www.cryonicsarchive.org/docs/cryonics-magazine-2016-04.pdf#page=28"><strong><u>Ralph Merkle</u></strong></a></p></blockquote>

<p style="text-align: left;">While we will never be able to be as anti-fragile as the Bitcoin network is, OpenSats strives to be as resilient as an organization can be. Our large board and passthrough commitment are a testament to that, as our founder mentioned above. The goal is to support our grantees for the long term in a sustainable way, which is why recurring donations are vital to what we do. So if you have a minute to <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/monthly"><strong><u>set up a recurring donation</u></strong></a>, please consider doing so.</p>

<h2 style="text-align: left;"><strong>Code Red: All Hands on Deck</strong></h2>

<p style="text-align: left;">A smooth sea never made a skilled sailor, or so the saying goes. One of the criticisms leveled at Bitcoin was that it is too boring. The institutions are here. Bitcoin is gradually being adopted as an asset. Ergo, nothing exciting is happening. Calm seas. Smooth sailing.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">The weather changed at the end of July, and the smooth sea turned into a maelstrom in the aftermath of vulnerabilities found by <code>kimi-k3</code> and friends. As mentioned in <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/blog/ongoing-exploits-of-bitcoin-open-source-software"><strong><u>one of our blog posts</u></strong></a>, parts of our infrastructure were exposed to these vulnerabilities as well, prompting us to take action to protect donor and grantee funds. As people started red teaming Bitcoin software and racking up costs, the OpenSats board decided to launch a new fund and grant program as a response: the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/funds/red"><strong><u>Red Team fund</u></strong></a> and <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/blog/code-red-supporting-first-responders"><strong><u>token reimbursement for red-teaming</u></strong></a>, respectively. Our job is to send sats to where they're needed most, which is why we decided to <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/blog/helping-the-helpers"><strong><u>help those that are already helping</u></strong></a>. We announced the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/blog/red-team-grants-first-set"><strong><u>first round of grants</u></strong></a> shortly after, and have been evaluating security-related grant applications ever since.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">While it was devastating to watch wallets of fellow bitcoiners being drained, it was at the same time inspiring to see so many people come together to fight the various fires and do everything in their power to make sure that users' funds are safe. As our founder mentioned in his letter above, OpenSats is purpose-built to weather storms like these: no benevolent dictator, <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/blog/avoiding-single-points-of-failure"><strong><u>no single point of failure</u></strong></a>.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">Sending sats directly to the people we fund—via the Bitcoin network, with no intermediaries—is what makes operating at our scale possible in the first place. In the last couple of months <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/tags/grants"><strong><u>we gave grants to people</u></strong></a> in Bangladesh, Kenya, Cameroon, Liberia, Albania, Singapore, and Paraguay, just to name a few. Bitcoin truly is a global movement, as a glance at the map of OpenSats grantees shows.</p>

<h2 style="text-align: left;"><strong>Sats Sent</strong></h2>

<p style="text-align: left;">Even with all these exploits going on, Bitcoin continues to function, unbothered. Every 10 minutes a new block is found. Every 10 minutes transactions are settled. Efficient, permissionless, trustless.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">In Q3 2026, OpenSats sent over 33 BTC, equivalent to over $2.2 million, to support open-source work. The transaction fees for these payouts were <code>26,599</code> sats, or $19.09. We paid the equivalent of <strong>0.0008% in transaction fees</strong>. The unfailing operation of the Bitcoin network is what makes our work possible.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">In Q3, July was our strongest month in terms of payouts, with <code>1,273,064,984</code> sats sent, followed by August and September with <code>1,115,030,189</code> sats and <code>937,341,097</code> sats respectively.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">In total, we sent <code>3,325,436,270</code> sats during the quarter, equivalent to $2,220,911.53. The <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/funds/red"><strong><u>Red Team Fund</u></strong></a> awarded $209,500 in grants and paid out $10,233 in Q3. Some grants awarded in Q3 are being paid out in Q4.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">Have a look at our <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/docs/financials/2026-Q3.pdf"><strong><u>Q3 financial statements</u></strong></a> for the fund breakdown, visit our <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/transparency"><strong><u>transparency page</u></strong></a> for more reports, or browse our <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/projects"><strong><u>project pages</u></strong></a> to see how many sats each listed project has received in total.</p>

<h2 style="text-align: left;"><strong>Built for the Long Term</strong></h2>

<p style="text-align: left;">Operationally speaking, 2026-Q3 was one of the roughest quarters for OpenSats. Navigating a sea of vulnerabilities while running an organization that is built upon open-source software and infrastructure—infrastructure that is being attacked without pause—has been challenging.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">We want OpenSats to be as resilient as possible, which is why we rely on a wide array of people to provide input and make decisions about funding and organizational changes.</p>

<figure><a href="https://opensats.org/blog/avoiding-single-points-of-failure" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><img src="https://opensats.org/static/images/transparency/funding-decisions.jpg" alt="Funding Decisions" width="640" style="max-width:100%;height:auto;" draggable="false"></a><figcaption></figcaption></figure>

<p style="text-align: left;">Speaking of changes: we believe that our grant programs must adapt to the new reality being shaped by agentic systems. Coding agents can be an accelerant if they are wielded with expertise and care. High-agency people can now do more, build more, and move the needle more than ever. We want to support those people as best as we can, and we want to make sure that highly motivated people have the means to work on freedom tech for the long term. We'll share more thoughts on this topic in an upcoming blog post.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">The <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/mission"><strong><u>mission of OpenSats</u></strong></a> remains the same: support and maintain a sustainable ecosystem of funding for free and open-source projects and contributors, especially Bitcoin-related projects and projects that help Bitcoin flourish. Sustainability implies resilience; anything that isn't resilient to change (or attack, for that matter) won't be around for the long term. To make sure that we will be, one of our main focus points in Q4 will be working towards more resilience across the board: more recurring funding, more experts joining our evaluation committees, less reliance on third-party systems, as well as more redundancy and fault tolerance.</p>

<h2 style="text-align: left;"><strong>The Great Hardening</strong></h2>

<p style="text-align: left;">It seems that new vulnerabilities are being found and exploited every day right now. Software that has been audited by hundreds, even thousands of people. Software that has been around for decades; software that most of the internet relies upon.</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">Finding vulnerabilities is painful. However, you can't fix what you haven't found, so from an open-source ecosystem perspective, all these exploits will eventually, hopefully, leave us with stronger software, better testing, and more resilient systems. Yes, right now it's mayhem out there. But the only way out is through, or, as the saying goes: &quot;If you're going through hell, keep going.&quot;</p>

<p style="text-align: left;">Developers and security researchers who are red-teaming Bitcoin software are at the forefront of this battle. We want to do what we can to support them through our <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://opensats.org/funds/red"><strong><u>Red Team Fund</u></strong></a>.</p>
